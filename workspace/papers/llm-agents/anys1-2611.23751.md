# Thinking Fast, Anywhere: One System-1 for the Decision Points of Any Agent Workflow

**Rui Li**<sup>1</sup> **Liyang He**<sup>1</sup> **Zheng Zhang**<sup>2</sup> **Zhenya Huang**<sup>1</sup> **Qi Liu**<sup>1 1</sup>University of Science and Technology of China <sup>2</sup>Nanyang Technological University ruili2000@mail.ustc.edu.cn [https://middle-chunjie.github.io/AnyS1/](https://middle-chunjie.github.io/AnyS1/)

## Abstract

Autonomous LLM agents incur substantial cost and latency by invoking a large model (System-2) at every step, even when many intermediate actions choose among a small set of known candidates. Existing dual-system agents offload such decisions to a lightweight System-1 model, but rely on workflow-specific hooks authored per environment. We present AnyS1, a framework that turns human-authored procedural skills—whose instructions naturally specify where choices arise and which options apply—into a shared, domain-general source of decision points. Offline, AnyS1 mines grounded enumerable decisions across over 1,000 public skills, gathers execution-grounded trajectory states inside isolated containers to construct Any-DP, and trains a single calibrated 4B pointer System-1 with label smoothing. At run time, compiled workflows resolve routine decisions in one forward pass and fall back to System-2 when confidence falls below a calibrated threshold. Across three unseen benchmarks and distinct System-2 model families, AnyS1 preserves task reliability while cutting episode cost by 75–91% and model-call time by 63–92% relative to an uncompiled System-2 agent, and maintains performance under zeroshot threshold transfer.

## 1 Introduction

Driven by advances in reasoning and tool use, large language models (LLMs) have evolved into autonomous agents capable of complex, long-horizon tasks (Kwa et al., 2025)—resolving repositorylevel software issues (Yang et al., 2024), operating desktop and web interfaces (Xie et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024), and handling policy-constrained customer workflows (Yao et al., 2025; Barres et al., 2025). Yet mainstream agent architectures invoke a large model (System-2) at every step over a growing trajectory (Yao et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025), causing inference latency and cost to escalate rapidly (Kapoor et al., 2025)—even though

many intermediate steps choose among a small set of known options rather than requiring open-ended generation (§4.4).

Typed decision models offer a fast alternative for such bounded choices (TypeSafe AI, 2026; Palmer, 2026): given the current state, a question, and candidate options, they output an option distribution in a single forward pass, acting as a fast, intuitive System-1 alongside System-2 for planning, open generation, and uncertain fallbacks (Kahneman, 2011). While recent dual-system agents use this collaboration to substantially cut decision latency and cost in search and specialized tool harnesses (Cao et al., 2026; Zhu et al., 2026; Wu and Lim, 2026), they remain restricted to decision points manually pre-defined for a single fixed workflow, collecting training supervision exclusively at those hard-coded hooks (Figure 1(a)). When a general agent faces diverse tasks governed by new workflows without pre-declared hooks, every judgment falls back to System-2.

We observe that general agents increasingly acquire workflows through agent skills (SKILL.md; Anthropic, 2025), a paradigm uniting flexibility with procedurality. Across public repositories of over a hundred thousand skills (FayeZC, 2026; Wang et al., 2026b), an agent can dynamically load relevant skills on demand, while each skill specifies a structured procedure whose branches and checks implicitly define where to judge and among which options (Figure 1(b)). Procedural skills thus offer a natural, workflow-agnostic source from which decision points can be mined automatically for dualsystem execution.

Building on this insight, we propose AnyS1, a framework that mines actionable decision points from procedural skills and extends single-LLM execution into calibrated dual-system collaboration (Figure 2). ## 2 Related Work

**Agent skills and workflow compilation.** Agent skills package procedural knowledge as instructions and resources loaded on demand without weight updates (Anthropic, 2025), extending executable skill libraries learned in open-ended play (Wang et al., 2024). One line of work studies how agents retrieve, follow and internalize skills (Xia et al., 2026; Lu et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026a; Dong et al., 2026; Cho and Park, 2026), or synthesizes interaction trajectories and verifiable tasks from skill corpora to fine-tune generative policies (Zeng et al., 2024; Song et al., 2024; Tan et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026a,b); a complementary line compiles natural-language procedures into explicit programs (Khattab et al., 2024), turning SOPs into executable pseudo-code (Yu et al., 2026), skills into runtime interfaces or state machines (Chen et al., 2026; Xu et al., 2026a; Li, 2026), and recurring paths into scheduled workflows, deterministic rules or neurosymbolic programs (Xiang et al., 2026; Weber and Taneja, 2026; Liu et al., 2026; Suh et al., 2026; Mazza and Levi, 2026; Wang and Zang, 2026). However, prior skill-learning methods treat each

**Typed decision models and calibrated deferral.** Inspired by dual-process theory (Kahneman, 2011) and LLM cascades (Chen et al., 2024; Ong et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026b), early fast–slow agents pair a small text generator or modular executor with a slow planner (Lin et al., 2023; Yin et al., 2024; Prasad et al., 2024; Saha et al., 2025; Su et al., 2025), whereas typed decision models cast System-1 as a non-generative scorer returning option probabilities in one forward pass. Dual-system agents built on these models accelerate search (Cao et al., 2026) and tool use (Wu and Lim, 2026; Zhu et al., 2026) via selective deferral to an LLM (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017; Sowietzki et al., 2026; Hemadri et al., 2026), with recent studies analyzing delegation boundaries and biases (Vats, 2026; Li et al., 2026b; Lukassen et al., 2026; Sun et al., 2026). ## 3.1 Problem Formulation

**From single- to dual-system execution.** Consider an agent executing a task by following a skill over a multi-step episode: at each step t, the agent observes the current state $s _ { t }$ (accumulated context, tool outputs and user messages) and produces an action or decision. A conventional single-system agent queries the LLM (System-2) at every step to reason and generate the output. To turn this into dual-system execution, we draw on dual-process theory (Kahneman, 2011) and classify workflow steps by task type and decision complexity into three node types: a D node (deterministic) is fully determined by structured state (e.g., a fixed tool call) and runs directly as code; a G node (generative) produces open-ended output and stays with System-2; and an E node (enumerable) reads unstructured content in state s and selects one option from a finite candidate set fixed in the skill or listable from $\mathcal { S } _ { z }$ making it suited to System-1 delegation. We formalize each skill-specified E node as a decision point template $( q , O , d )$ : a question $q ,$ options $O = \{ o _ { 1 } , \ldots , o _ { n } \}$ with one-sentence definitions $d ( o )$ , and none of the above; triggered at state s, it forms a runtime instance $x = ( s , q , O , d )$

**System-1 and delegation.** To resolve enumerable E nodes without the latency and cost of autoregressive generation, we introduce a lightweight typed decision model f (System-1) that maps x in one forward pass to a distribution $p _ { f } ( \cdot \mid x )$ over $O ,$ predicting $\hat { y } \; = \; \operatorname { a r g } \operatorname { m a x } _ { o \in O } p _ { f } ( o \; \mid \; x )$ Because a compact f has limited capacity and encounters unseen skills or ambiguous states where a wrong branch can derail downstream trajectories, we selectively delegate only high-confidence judgments to System-1: at each E node, a delegation rule $g ( x ) \in \{ 0 , 1 \}$ decides whether to adopt $\hat { y } \left( g ( x ) = 1 \right)$ or fall back to System- $- 2 \left( g ( x ) = 0 \right)$ where $All - S2 (g \equiv  0 )$ and All-S1 $( g   \equiv   1 )$ correspond to relying solely on System-2 or System-1 as the two extremes.

**Objective.** To formalize how selective delegation reduces inference cost while preserving task success, let C and $C ^ { \mathrm { s 2 } }$ denote the per-episode costs of a dual-system configuration and All-S2. Splitting C into System-2 cost at G and E nodes and System-1 cost gives

$$
\frac { C } { C ^ { \mathrm { S 2 } } } \; = \; \underbrace { \frac { C _ { G } } { C ^ { \mathrm { S 2 } } } } _ { \approx   1 - s _ { E } } \; + \; \underbrace { \frac { C _ { E } } { C ^ { \mathrm { S 2 } } } } _ { \leq   s _ { E } } \; + \; \underbrace { \frac { C _ { \mathrm { S 1 } } } { C ^ { \mathrm { S 2 } } } } _ { \kappa } ,\tag{1}
$$

## 3.2 Skill-Grounded Data Synthesis: Any-DP

To train a general System-1, we construct Any-DP by first mining decision point templates $( q , O , d )$ from unstructured skill prose (SKILL.md) and then executing synthesized tasks in isolated containers to collect authentic runtime states s and consensus labels $y \in O$

**Extracting decision points from skills.** To ensure that mined decision points reflect genuine workflow branches, for each skill an LLM analyzer independently reads SKILL.md and its bundled documents three times, returning typed candidate decision points with options and a verbatim supporting quote verified via exact string matching against the skill text. We then call an LLM to merge equivalent points across the three runs, record support $\sigma ( p ) \in \{ 1 , 2 , 3 \}$ , take the majority value per field, and write a one-sentence option definition $d ( o )$ from the skill text to disambiguate identically named options. We retain E nodes with $\sigma ( p ) \; \geq \; 2$ that are answerable from state alone, yielding grounded templates $( q , O , d )$

**Synthesizing executable tasks from skills.** Extracted templates $( q , O , d )$ specify what to judge, yet training System-1 also requires states s that reflect genuine runtime contexts: asking an LLM to fabricate states directly yields clean prose summaries that fail to generalize to fragmented tool outputs along real trajectories. To automatically elicit authentic deep-branch states across diverse skills, for each skill (split beforehand at the repository level into training and held-out sets to pre-vent leakage) an LLM synthesizes self-contained multi-stage tasks complete with input files, a reference solution, and a hidden verifier, paired with two cross-category distractor skills that require the agent to both select the right skill and advance across dependent stages; a task is retained only if, inside a network-isolated container, the verifier rejects the initial files and accepts the reference solution.

**Trajectory execution and state labeling.** To capture authentic interaction contexts, we use an Open-Hands agent (Wang et al., 2025) to execute each verified task in the isolated container, after which an LLM locator aligns the trajectory to the skill’s decision points and extracts verbatim the preceding observations (task instructions, user messages, and raw tool outputs) at the step where each decision arises. Because the executing agent’s own actions may be suboptimal and single-pass LLM teachers exhibit option-order bias, we query two independent teacher passes per extracted state s (reversing option order in the second pass) and retain

**Synthesized dataset Any-DP.** Running this pipeline on 1,072 public skills from SkillMD-138K (FayeZC, 2026)—spanning 16 categories such as web front-end, software engineering, and data analysis—yields Any-DP, comprising 17,865 typed decision points, 2,846 container-verified tasks, and 25,986 consensus-labeled examples (Table 1). ## 3.3 Calibrated System-1 Training and Runtime Deferral

**Model architecture and calibrated objective.** We build System-1 by attaching an optioninteraction pointer head to Qwen3.5-4B-Base (Qwen Team, 2026) and training it on Any-DP. Given a runtime instance $x \; = \; ( s , q , O , d )$ with $O = \{ o _ { 1 } , \ldots , o _ { n } \}$ , we serialize x using structural delimiters into a single input sequence:

$$
\begin{array} { r l } & { \tilde { x } = \big [ \langle \mathrm { s } \rangle , s ;   \langle \mathrm { q } \rangle , q ; } \\ & { \qquad \big ( \langle \mathrm { o p t } \rangle , o _ { i } ; d ( o _ { i } ) , \langle / \mathrm { o p t } \rangle \big ) _ { i = 1 } ^ { n } ;   \langle \mathrm { d e c } \rangle \big ] . } \end{array}\tag{2}
$$

From one forward pass over x˜, this head passes the ⟨dec⟩ hidden state and the n $\langle / \mathrm { o p t } \rangle$ hidden states through a position-free bidirectional self-attention layer so candidate options contextualize one another, then matches each option against ⟨dec⟩ to output $p _ { f } ( o \mid x )$ via softmax (Appendix B). Be-

cause runtime deferral relies on $p _ { f } ( o   \mid   x )$ to reflect true correctness likelihood on unseen skills, whereas hard-label cross-entropy drives overconfident predictions (Guo et al., 2017), we build calibration directly into training via label smoothing (Müller et al., 2019):

$$
\mathcal { L } = \sum _ { ( x , y ) \in \mathcal { D } } \operatorname { C E } \bigl ( p _ { f } ( \cdot \mid x ) , \thinspace ( 1 { - } \epsilon ) \thinspace e _ { y } { + } \epsilon \thinspace u _ { O } \bigr ) ,\tag{3}
$$

where $e _ { y }$ is the one-hot label, $u _ { O }$ is uniform over O, and $\epsilon = 0 . 1$

**Confidence-gated deferral.** To prevent System-1 errors on unfamiliar states from derailing downstream execution, the runtime gate delegates an E decision to System-1 only when its maximum probability $c(x) = \max_{o \in O} p_{f}(o \mid x)$ clears a threshold τ :

$$
g ( x ) = \mathbb { I } \big [ c ( x ) \geq \tau \big ] ,\tag{4}
$$

where τ is the smallest threshold achieving at least 95% agreement on calibration decisions with $c ( x ) ~ \geq ~ \tau .$ To support both brand-new workflows and workflows already in deployment, we determine τ under two regimes (fixed before any test run; Appendix C): (1) Cold-start transfer $( A n y S I _ { c o l d } ) { : }$ to operate with zero target-task executions, $\mathrm { A n y S 1 _ { c o l d } }$ fits a single global τ on 153 examples from held-out development skills and applies it unchanged to every benchmark; (2) Unlabeled development calibration $( A n y S I _ { w a r m } ) { : }$ to adapt τ to an active workflow without human annotation, $\mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { w a r m } }$ executes 50 disjoint development episodes with System-2 while recording System-1’s scores alongside (Zhu et al., 2026), fitting τ against System-2’s decisions.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate AnyS1 around four research questions. **RQ1**: Does AnyS1 cut cost and latency on unseen workflows without lowering task success? **RQ2**: How do the gate, executed states, and calibrated loss each contribute? **RQ3**: What governs the savings? **RQ4**: Does calibrated confidence select the right decisions to delegate?

**Benchmarks.** To test cross-domain generalization without retraining, we evaluate on three unseen agent benchmarks spanning policy-constrained dialogue, partially observable embodied search, and noisy web navigation, with zero skill overlap with

## 4.2 RQ1: Main Results

**Skill compilation and calibrated delegation provide complementary efficiency gains.** Table 2 separates two orthogonal levers behind end-to-end efficiency. First, compiling unstructured skills into explicit D/E/G workflows (All-S2) removes mechanical tool orchestration from LLM turns and arrests prompt accumulation across dialogue history, preventing step-skipping, lifting macro-average effectiveness by nearly 18 points, and saving 76–86% of the uncompiled agent’s episode cost (81% on average). Second, offloading routine E decisions to single-pass System-1 under AnyS1<sub>warm</sub> raises the average saving to 89% (86–91% across benchmarks) and cuts critical-path model time by 70–92% relative to the uncompiled agent (vs. 51–87% for All-S2), while matching All-S2 reliability on average (+0.5 avg. pt). In fact, single-pass local scoring suppresses verbose System-2 over-deliberation on straightforward binary relevance checks, improving WebShop score over All-S2 (68.1 vs. 66.9). Compared against commercial Jev, self-hosted 4B AnyS1 saves more of the uncompiled agent’s cost (89% vs. 84% on average) at lower per-episode latency by avoiding remote API round-trips. Even without target-workflow runs, cold-start $\mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { c o l d } }$ stays level with All-S2 (+0.1 avg. pt) while saving 87% of the uncompiled agent’s cost on average.

**A single System-1 generalizes across System-2 model families without retraining.** Replacing DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with GPT-6 Sol reproduces the same two-tier pattern with the unchanged System-1 weights (Table 2, bottom): compilation alone saves 71% of single-system Sol’s cost on average (56–86%) at +16.8 avg. pt, and $\mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { w a r m } }$ raises the saving to 82% (75–91%) while cutting model time by 63–84% (vs. 40–74% for All-S2). Cold-start $\mathrm { A n y S 1 _ { c o l d } }$ saves 81% within 0.8 avg. pt of All-S2 without any Sol trajectories. Because Sol has higher token pricing than Flash, fixed 4B inference accounts for an even smaller fraction of episode cost, underscoring that Any-DP captures workflow-intrinsic decision semantics rather than teacher-specific priors.

To isolate the role of each component, Table 3 evaluates ablations along three dimensions: (a) runtime deferral gating, (b) training state source, and (c) calibration objective (detailed setups in Appendix B).

**Confidence gating prevents cascading search failures in long horizons.** Comparing our calibrated gates $( \mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { w a r m } }$ and $\mathrm { A n y S 1 _ { c o l d } ) }$ against ungated All-S1—which delegates every E decision to System-1 unconditionally—shows that shorthorizon routing workflows remain intact without gating, whereas long-horizon ALFWorld exploration collapses $( 96.3\% ~ \rightarrow ~ 40.3\% )$ . Both

**Container-executed states bridge the observation gap left by synthetic summaries.** We compare Any-DP against LLM-written synthetic (S1-syn), which trains the same 4B architecture on LLM-drafted state summaries conditioned on mined decision points without container execution. Training on $\mathrm{Any-DP's}$ executed container states outperforms S1-syn across every split—most notably by +18.8 points on the held-out container development set (Dev) and +5.3 points on ALF-World E—plausibly because idealized synthetic summaries mismatch terse, truncated terminal outputs.

**Label smoothing prevents out-of-domain confidence saturation.** Replacing Eq. 3 with unsmoothed hard-label cross-entropy or $C E + B r i e r$ on the same Any-DP training set yields nearly unchanged raw accuracy yet degrades out-of-domain calibration (Table 3c). Because unsmoothed logits saturate near probability 1.0 even on unfamiliar states, the dev-fitted threshold inflates toward $\tau = 0 . 9 9 8$ and degrades cross-domain gated agreement (mean agreement drops from 97.7% to 92.4–93.7%), whereas label smoothing preserves uniform probability mass and a transferable confidence margin (Appendix C).

**Workflow E-node share governs both cost and latency ceilings.** Normalizing episode cost and time to the uncompiled agent via Eq. 1 (Figure 3) shows that post-compilation delegation targets the remaining E share: E nodes hold 17–22% of uncompiled cost and 11–18% of time on ALFWorld and WebShop, vs. 4% and 19% on SOPBench (G nodes: 10% and 30%). **Single-pass scoring converts delegation into critical-path speedup.** Locally served System-1 scores all options in 21.8–31.7 ms median (Appendix B), 56–81× faster than 1.51–1.78 s System-2 calls. ## 5 Conclusion

We presented AnyS1, turning on-demand procedural skills into a shared source of structured decision points without custom per-workflow System-1 engineering. By mining grounded enumerable judgments across public skills, collecting containerexecuted states, and training one label-smoothed pointer System-1, AnyS1 resolves routine steps and calibrates deferral to System-2 in one forward pass. Experiments across unseen benchmarks and distinct System-2 model families show that both unlabeled calibration and zero-shot threshold transfer preserve all-System-2 reliability at substantially lower cost and critical-path latency.

## Limitations

While $\mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { c o l d } }$ transfers with zero targetworkflow executions, achieving the largest cost reductions under $\mathrm { A n y S l } _ { \mathrm { w a r m } }$ uses about 50 development episodes per workflow in which System-2 answers every E node, and large episode-level cost savings rely on compiling procedural skills into explicit programs; adapting deferral thresholds online from deferred System-2 calls and automatically compiling open-ended repository skills are natural next steps.

## A Any-DP Construction, Prompts, and Worked Example

**Source curation and split isolation.** We curate procedural skills from SkillMD-138K (FayeZC, 2026) according to four criteria: (i) procedural structure and licensing, retaining multi-step English procedural documents released under permissive open-source SPDX licenses while excluding bulk aggregator repositories; (ii) diversity and deduplication, applying MinHash near-duplicate removal and capping at most three skills per repository so no single codebase dominates; (iii) split isolation and benchmark decontamination, partitioning repositories into disjoint training and held-out sets beforehand and excluding any skill that references downstream evaluation or public containerized agent benchmarks (SOPBench, ALFWorld, WebShop, SkillRL, or τ -bench); and (iv) sandbox executability, verifying that each skill can run endto-end inside an offline Linux container without external accounts or live human input. Stratified sampling across 16 functional categories yields the 1,072 public training skills in Any-DP.

**Decision-point filtering and task synthesis.** Across three extraction passes per skill (Figure 5, Prompt 1), an LLM merger retains E nodes with support $\sigma ( p ) ~ \geq ~ 2$ that express non-preference judgments, are answerable from local text context, and include at least two candidate options alongside none of the above. For skills with at least three retained E nodes, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash generates multi-stage Linux tasks paired with cross-category distractors, a hidden verifier, and a reference solution that passes inside an isolated container (Prompt 2).

**Execution labeling and dataset summary.** OpenHands agents (Wang et al., 2025) execute the verified tasks, a trajectory locator copies verbatim pre-decision observations (user messages and tool

outputs, excluding agent reasoning), and two orderreversed teacher passes assign consensus labels (Prompt 3). Overall, 2,846 verified tasks across 1,072 skills yield 17,865 mined decision points and 25,986 consensus examples (89.2% teacher agreement; median 3 options besides none of the above; 6.7% labeled none of the above). Applying the same labeling protocol to disjoint Apache-2.0 containerized trajectories gives the 153 calibration states $( \mathrm { A n y S 1 _ { c o l d } } )$ and 185 offline development states (Dev, Appendix C).

**Prompt templates and synthesized example.** Figure 5 shows the prompt templates for the three offline LLM stages used to build Any-DP, and Figure 6 illustrates an authentic end-to-end synthesized decision record from Any-DP, showing how a mined E-node template $( q , O , d )$ is grounded into a container-executed observation state s, serialized via Eq. 2, and labeled by order-reversed dual-teacher consensus.

## B Implementation, Training, and Evaluation Protocols

**Architecture and training hyperparameters.** We build System-1 on Qwen3.5-4B-Base (Qwen Team, 2026) using rank-16 LoRA adapters (Hu et al., 2022) $( \alpha = 3 2 )$ across attention, MLP, and Gated DeltaNet projections. ## C Additional Offline, Calibration, and Online Analyses

**Full offline baseline comparison.** Expanding Table 3(b), Table 4 evaluates System-1 (three seeds) across our offline development sets (Dev, SOPBench E, ALFWorld E, and its search subset) against zero-shot typed decision models (Kev-4B, AgentDecision-4B, commercial Jev), zeroshot Qwen3.5-4B/27B, and S1-syn. On heldout Dev, System-1 (83.8%) outperforms S1-syn (65.0%) and commercial Jev (78.4%), while the option-interaction head improves over a standard pointer head (82.7%) by +1.1 points; on ALF-World search, the stronger 4B models (Qwen3.5-4B, AgentDecision-4B, and both Any-DP heads) plateau at 38–44% due to missing room priors.

**Calibration objective analysis.** On Dev, our 4B System-1 achieves lower negative log-likelihood (NLL) than commercial Jev and AgentDecision-4B

(0.52 vs. 0.58 and 0.69) at 0.07 expected calibration error (ECE). Replacing label smoothing (ϵ = 0.1) with hard-label cross-entropy (ϵ = 0) or CE + Brier leaves offline accuracy nearly unchanged (within 0.5 points; Table 3c) while pushing unsmoothed predictions toward probability 1.0 even on unfamiliar states. Consequently, the threshold fitted for 95% agreement inflates near 1.0 and reduces three-set mean gated agreement from 97.7% to 92.4–93.7%.

**Confidence ranking vs. random delegation.** Comparing our single-pass confidence gate (c(x) ≥ τ ) against matched-rate random delegation across All-S2 E decisions (Table 5) confirms that uncertainty ranking—not lower delegation volume—drives accuracy, cutting AURC by 2–6× and reducing ALFWorld disagreements from 16.0 to 3.8 per 100 decisions.
